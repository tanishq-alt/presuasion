# Chapter 6: Commanders of Attention 2 — The Magnetizers

## Core Idea
Attention is a two-part machine: attracting it (ch5) and **holding** it. The communicator who fastens attention onto favorable elements wins by letting opposing views get locked out. Three magnetizers hold attention most reliably: **the self-relevant, the unfinished, and the mysterious.**

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Three Magnetizers** — self-relevance, unfinished states (Zeigarnik effect), and mysteries.
- **Zeigarnik Effect** — unfinished tasks are more memorable; cognition hoards attention on them until closure. Replicated in 600+ studies.
- **Next-in-Line Effect** — if you're about to speak (or just spoke), you miss what the people around you say. Self-focus hijacks attention. The person seated next to a key decision-maker is the worst place for their own message.
- **Craving for Cognitive Closure** — unanswered questions, unresolved problems, interrupted narratives drive a "gnawing desire" to complete them. The lever behind writing-session cliffhangers and lecture mysteries.

## Magnetizer 1: The Self-Relevant

**Information about yourself is attentional superglue.** Watch who people look at first, longest, and last in a group photo.

**Lever — the pronoun "you".** Replacing "people / they" with "you" in ad copy enhances attitude toward the product (Ohio State study). Caveat: self-relevance attracts attention but does *not* grant automatic approval. If the rest of the message is weak, now-attentive readers will reject it *harder*. "You" is a magnifier.

**Practical use in meetings:**
- Don't sit next to the key decision-maker.
- Sitting next-to triggers the next-in-line effect either way: if you speak first, they rehearse their own turn; if they speak first, they rehash. Either way they miss your pitch.
- **Sit across from them** — far enough for the next-in-line effect to clear, visually prominent enough to get both attention and perceived causality (what's focal is causal, ch4).
- Corollary: if your idea is *weak*, sit next-to. Their self-focus will shield you.

## Magnetizer 2: The Unfinished

**Origin story:** Lewin and his student Bluma Zeigarnik watched a Berlin beer-garden waiter flawlessly remember all orders — then *forget* them completely moments after serving the last dish. Completion released the memory; the task-state had been the attention-holder.

**Two conclusions from the Zeigarnik effect:**
1. Everything about an unfinished committed-to task is remembered *better* than a finished one.
2. Interrupting a committed task produces discomfort and pull-back-to-it — a craving for closure.

**Applications:**
- **Advertising:** TV commercials cut 5–6 seconds before their natural ending produced the *highest* recall of their details. Measured immediately, 2 days later, and especially 2 weeks later. Non-closure holds memory.
- **Attraction:** college women preferred the attractive male raters whose ratings *weren't yet revealed* over those who had rated them highest. Unknown outcomes keep popping up in mind → repeated attention → attribution of higher attractiveness.
- **Writing productivity** (Cialdini's colleague): never finish a session at the end of a paragraph or thought. Stop mid-paragraph with the next sentence pre-known but unwritten. The craving for closure yanks you back to the chair tomorrow.
- **Project management:** leave the ugly last 10% intentionally open, signaled, and on-deck. The Zeigarnik state pulls the team back.

## Magnetizer 3: The Mysterious

Cialdini's own discovery: when researching his first popular book, he sorted sections of academic-written popular books into "worked" and "didn't work" piles. The worked pile shared one unexpected feature: **they opened with a mystery.** The authors described a perplexing state of affairs and invited the reader in to resolve it.

**Why mysteries beat descriptions and questions:**
- Descriptions require *notice*.
- Questions require *answers*.
- Mysteries require **explanations.** Explanations require integration of all relevant facts → deeper learning → better test scores.

**Einstein (recounted by Cialdini): "The most beautiful thing we can experience is the mysterious. It is the source of all true science and art."**

**The Six-Step Mystery Structure for persuasive teaching:**
1. **Pose the mystery.** Present a puzzling fact. (Tobacco companies successfully lobbied to *ban* their own TV/radio ads in 1971. Why?)
2. **Deepen the mystery.** Add corroborating-but-confusing evidence. (The move was aggressive and against the broadcast industry's wishes.)
3. **Consider and eliminate alternative explanations.** Public health concern? No — they moved marketing to print, billboards, and film. Broadcast industry agreed? No — they sued.
4. **Provide a clue.** The FCC's "fairness doctrine" required equal broadcast time for opposing views on controversial issues — only on radio and TV.
5. **Resolve the mystery.** Anti-tobacco counter-ads (American Cancer Society) got free airtime equal to tobacco ads. Counter-ads cut consumption 10%. By banning their own ads from the air, tobacco companies killed the free counter-ad slot and jumped sales *and* cut spending by a third.
6. **Draw the implication.** *Counterarguments are typically more powerful than arguments.* The best way to enhance acceptance of your message is to reduce the availability of counterarguments to it.

## Why Mystery Structure Works
Attention is magnetized not just to topic but to detail. To solve a mystery, observers must weigh every fact. So the usually-skipped particulars get real cognitive processing. Audiences retain *more*, understand *deeper*, and are harder to interrupt. Cialdini once accidentally ran out of lecture time before revealing a mystery's solution — his students refused to leave the room until he resolved it.

## Mental Models
- **The superglue triangle.** Self-relevance + unfinished + mysterious. The three magnetize attention. Any one is strong; combined, nearly inescapable.
- **The attention menu has two courses.** Attracting (ch5) and holding (ch6). Ads over-serve the first; teachers under-serve the second.
- **"I enjoy *having written*."** (Maugham.) The pleasure of closure is the lever — *denying* yourself that pleasure productively is the move.
- **Mystery > question.** Questions get answers. Mysteries get explanations. Explanations build models.

## Anti-patterns
- **Using "you" without a strong case to follow.** Self-relevance without merit backfires harder than a neutral opener.
- **Sitting next to the decision-maker to "connect."** Next-in-line effect wipes out your message.
- **Finishing writing sessions at clean ends.** Kills the closure craving that brings you back.
- **Opening a pitch or lecture with a description or question.** Lower retention than opening with a mystery.
- **Delivering a counterargument that attacks the *claim*, not the source.** Attacks on the specific point win that round; attacks on the source's *trustworthiness* win all future rounds.

## Worked Example — The Tobacco Industry's 1971 Self-Ban
See the six-step structure above. The key teaching: in July 1969, tobacco companies *publicly advocated* for the ban that took their own ads off US TV and radio by 1971. They did it to kill the fairness-doctrine-mandated free counter-advertising. Within a year of the ban, sales were up and ad spending was down ~33%. The lesson they bought at scale: counterarguments beat arguments; silence the opposition and your weaker case wins.

## Key Takeaways
1. Attracting attention gets you in the room. *Holding* attention closes the deal. Magnetizers do the holding.
2. Self-relevance ("you") is the strongest ordinary lever — but it magnifies, doesn't invent, value.
3. Zeigarnik — the lever behind cliffhangers, half-done writing, and refusing closure deliberately to hold the next session's focus.
4. Mysteries outperform descriptions and questions because they recruit *explanation*, which demands integration of detail.
5. In rooms with decision-makers: sit across, not next to. Visibility + distance = perceived causality + preserved attention.
6. Silencing counterarguments is more powerful than amplifying your own argument.
