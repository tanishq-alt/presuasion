# Chapter 1: Pre-Suasion — An Introduction

## Core Idea
The best persuaders spend more time on what they do *before* making a request than on the request itself. **Pre-suasion** is the process of arranging for recipients to be receptive to a message before they encounter it. Timing + the *opener* that precedes the ask can carry equal or greater weight than the merits of the ask itself.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Pre-suasion** — the process of arranging recipients to be receptive to a message *before* they encounter it. "To persuade optimally, it's necessary to pre-suade optimally."
  - When to use: before any important ask, pitch, proposal, negotiation.
  - How: craft what you say *just before* the message so that attention and associations in the listener's mind favor your case.

- **Openers** — Cialdini's umbrella term for the pre-suasive device (also called frames, anchors, primes, mindsets, first impressions). They do two jobs: initiate the process *and* remove existing barriers.

- **Privileged Moments** — specific points in time when an individual is *particularly receptive* to a communicator's message. A privileged moment carries (a) a time-limited window of opportunity and (b) leveraging force from physics — an instant of unusual psychological swing.

- **The Big Same** — six universal principles of persuasion (from *Influence*, Cialdini's earlier book): **reciprocation, liking, social proof, authority, scarcity, consistency**. Chapter 11 adds a seventh: **unity**.

## Key Concepts
- **Moment monitor vs. moment maker** — the practitioner can either *recognize* privileged moments arising naturally or *create* them (more ethically perilous).
- **Opener as door-opener** — the first function is to start the message; the second is to remove the listener's protective barriers (illustrated by Jim the salesman literally being handed a door key).
- **The "big initial number" effect** — mentioning a large number ("I can't charge you a million dollars for this") anchors the subsequent real figure as modest. Also: Studio 97 vs Studio 17, high vs low jersey numbers.
- **Likelihood, not certainty** — no technique works every time; the lever is a meaningful increase in odds across many occasions.

## Mental Models
- **Garden metaphor** — even great seeds won't take root in poorly prepared ground. Top persuaders cultivate the soil (the pre-suasive opener), then plant the seed (the message).
- **Oh, the moment!** — contrast with Cicero's "Oh, the times! Oh, the customs!" Cialdini argues the *moment* is a more immediate, manageable lever than the era or norms.
- **What's presented first changes how what comes next is experienced.** Not one specific stimulus — numbers, line-lengths, music, or any selected psychological concept.

## Anti-patterns
- **Relying on the merits of your offer alone.** Even strong offers lose to prepared ground.
- **Padding the budget for pushback.** Cialdini's consultant example — padding felt wrong; the pre-suasive anchor ("not a million dollars") worked better.
- **Making a request in isolation.** The associate dean story: Cialdini accepted a huge teaching ask only because it came inside the privileged moment of gratitude. Had it come the next day, "no" would have been easy.
- **Treating persuasion as artistic inspiration.** It's lawful and learnable; procedures replicate across situations.

## Worked Example — Jim the Fire-Alarm Salesman
The top performer at a home fire-alarm company didn't modify his standard pitch or product. Everyone else carried the demonstration book into the home upfront. Jim did not — he waited until the couple had started a ten-minute written fire-safety test, then slapped his forehead: *"I forgot some really important information in my car, and I need to get it. I don't want to interrupt the test — would you mind if I let myself out and back into your home?"* Often he was handed a door key.

His own explanation: *"Who do you let walk in and out of your house on their own? Only somebody you trust. I want to be associated with trust in those families' minds."*

He didn't *claim* trust — he arranged to be *treated* in a way associated with trust. The association did the rest. That was the only material difference Cialdini could find between Jim's presentations and the less successful reps'.

## Worked Example — The $75,000 / $1 Million Anchor
A Toronto consultant kept losing 10–15% of his fee to price pushback. One day, before stating his $75,000 fee, he joked: *"As you can tell, I'm not going to be able to charge you a million dollars for this."* The client laughed and said *"Well, I can agree to that!"* No subsequent discussion of fee. A large anchor-number — even one obviously unrealistic — reframed $75k as modest. He has used it since; it almost always eliminates challenges to the charges.

## Key Takeaways
1. The question isn't *what* to say — it's *what to say just before* what you want to say.
2. The frame around a request can matter more than the request's content.
3. Openers can be anchors (numbers), associations (trust), primes (concepts), or situational (music/geography) — any of these change downstream judgment.
4. Privileged moments are time-limited. If you miss the window, the same request made the next day fails.
5. Persuasion is lawful, replicable, and learnable. Science, not art.
6. Ethics matter: pre-suasive practices can inform *or* exploit. Choose one (ch13).
