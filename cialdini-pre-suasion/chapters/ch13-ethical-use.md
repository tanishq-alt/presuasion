# Chapter 13: Ethical Use — A Pre-Pre-Suasive Consideration

## Core Idea
Before deciding *what* to say or do just before an appeal, there's a prior decision: whether to pursue success this way at all. The traditional "reputational damage" case against dishonesty is correct but insufficient — ~half of executives will act unethically anyway because they don't expect to get caught. **The real deterrent is internal:** unethical cultures produce three self-inflicted tumors — poor performance, high turnover, and employee fraud — that damage profitability *even when no one outside notices.*

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Reputation Argument (traditional, insufficient alone).** Public exposure of misconduct → reputational harm → lost market value. Real but relies on the operator expecting to get caught. Most don't.
- **The Triple-Tumor Structure of Organizational Dishonesty** (Cialdini, Li, Samper). Three malignant internal consequences: (1) poor employee performance, (2) high employee turnover, (3) employee fraud and malfeasance. Hard to trace to unethical culture via standard accounting.
- **Moral Stress** — conflict between an employee's ethical values and perceived organizational values. More damaging than typical workplace stressors (difficult customers, unsupportive supervisors, task conflict).
- **"Those who cheat for you will cheat against you."** Culture of misconduct retains the misconduct-comfortable and expels the ethical — the residue cheats the company.
- **Untrustworthiness** — the single worst concept to be associated with (from ch7 foreshadowing). Ethically centered chapter's confirmation.

## Why Reputational Argument Alone Fails
- Market value loss is well-documented (2005 SEC study: 585 firms lost ~41% of market value after public misconduct, ~2/3 from reputational damage).
- 80% of Americans say ethical perception directly affects their purchases.
- VW's 2015 emissions scandal: sales dropped to 1/16 industry average, largest annual loss in company history, reputation flipped from 70% favorable to 80% unfavorable.
- But: global surveys show ~half of senior executives would act unethically to win business, knowing the risks.
- Why? **They don't expect to get caught.** Crime-deterrence literature: those who commit high-penalty violations typically do so believing detection is unlikely.

## The Triple Tumors

### 1. Poor Employee Performance
**Lab experiment.** Business students joined team doing an online problem-solving task. All got "67% correct." Half the teams had a leader announce: "I'll report 80% to improve our standing — no way the researcher will catch us." The other half reported honestly. All subjects then took an individual critical-reasoning test.
- **The dishonest-team group scored 20% lower on the individual test.**
- They also *stopped working sooner* — less energy, less motivation.

**National survey of workers** (~3 years tenure):
- More unethical the climate → poorer job performance.
- More unethical the climate → more felt stress.
- The stress *caused* the poor performance. Moral stress is a serious degrader of competence.

### 2. Employee Turnover
Direct turnover costs: 50% of annual compensation (low-level) to 200%+ (executive). Midsize firm, 1000 employees, even 10% voluntary turnover at $40k average package = $4M/year.

**Lab finding:** 51% of participants in *ethical* teams asked to leave vs. **80% of participants in unethical teams** asked to leave.

**National survey finding:** employees in unethical organizations felt more stress, and that stress drove the desire to quit.

### 3. Employee Fraud and Malfeasance
**The viper logic:** honest employees leave; those comfortable with dishonesty stay; those who stay cheat the company. Global cost of employee fraud: trillions of dollars (embezzlement, inventory theft, expense inflation, kickbacks, sabotage).

**Lab finding:** after the earlier dishonesty exposure, subjects were told they'd be in competition against teammates for a $100 lottery; they "accidentally" saw the answer key.
- Participants who had stayed in *honest* teams: cheating ~nonexistent.
- Participants who had left either team: moderate, non-significant cheating.
- **Participants who had stayed in dishonest teams: cheated 77% more than everyone else's average.**

**Survey finding:** employees in unethical workplaces who preferred to stay were abnormally likely to alter expense reports, sabotage equipment, misuse company resources.

**MBA student essay (Cialdini's class)** about a $1B-loss company:
> "The CEO claimed scarcity when there was abundance, used authority to make others act against their better judgment, created social proof that didn't exist. People believed him short-term, but as the truth leaked, reputation deteriorated. The marketing department exaggerated, PR wrote false press releases, salespeople coerced customers. Dissatisfaction and turnover were incredibly high. Employees stole from the company via expense reports and side deals with suppliers. When I visited a few months ago, nearly half the staff had left; morale was lower than ever."

## Three Operational Recommendations
1. **Honesty ratings by customers/clients** of employees they interact with → part of employees' incentive structures.
2. **Ethical reputation of the company as a whole** → measured and included in yearly performance assessments.
3. **Employee ratings of the firm's ethical orientation** → part of senior management's (especially the CEO's) compensation.

Why these three: they not only incentivize ethical conduct, they **bring continuing focused attention to ethical standards** — which, per earlier chapters, elevates perceived importance and causal weight of ethics in organizational decisions.

## Mental Models
- **Ethics ≠ moral finger-wagging.** The chapter reframes ethics as a *profitability* argument internal to the firm.
- **The viper the firm invites under its coat.** Residue effect — selection pressure on who stays.
- **Moral stress is a measurable stressor.** Not a soft concept; shows up in fatigue, burnout, performance drops.
- **Attention to ethics (via measurement) is itself pre-suasive.** Measuring = focusing = elevating importance.
- **The ethical opener for self.** Buffett admits mistakes to pre-suade trust; the ethical culture admits standards to pre-suade compliance from the inside.

## Anti-patterns
- **Relying only on the reputational argument.** Half of executives will discount it because they expect non-detection.
- **"Short-term profit from dishonesty" as a coherent strategy.** Three internal tumors cost more than the gain, and they don't require outside detection.
- **Hiring "cheaters for the company."** They cheat the company.
- **Moralizing against misconduct.** Doesn't move business decisions. Translate to currency.
- **Compartmentalizing ethics as HR-only.** Measurement in the CEO's compensation puts it where power lies.
- **Pre-suasion techniques in pursuit of a case that's false.** From earlier chapters: the lever only amplifies the material under it; using it for lies accelerates detection and the tumors.

## Worked Example — The $1B Loss Company (student essay)
See block quote above. The CEO used every influence principle inauthentically (scarcity-without-scarcity, authority-overriding-judgment, fabricated social proof). The dishonesty cascaded department by department. Short-term wins from believed claims. Long-term: reputation collapse, honest-employee flight, systematic internal cheating, 50%+ staff loss. Dishonesty is a *compounding* negative in the firm, not a trade-off.

## Key Takeaways
1. Reputational damage is real but insufficient as a deterrent — most don't expect to get caught.
2. The three internal tumors (performance, turnover, fraud) damage the firm even without external detection.
3. Moral stress is a measurable, performance-destroying stressor.
4. Unethical cultures self-select for cheating employees, who then cheat the firm.
5. Make ethics measurable: customer ratings of employees, firm-wide ethics rating, employee-ratings of leadership in the CEO's compensation.
6. Pre-suasion is a lever on *truth* — applying it to false cases accelerates the lever's eventual collapse.
7. Buffett/Munger-style admitted-weakness openness is more than politeness: it's a sustainable persuasion strategy that produces the opposite of the tumors.
