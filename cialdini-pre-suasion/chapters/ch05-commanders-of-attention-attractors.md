# Chapter 5: Commanders of Attention 1 — The Attractors

## Core Idea
Some cues seize attention automatically — no effort required from the communicator — because they tap survival-level drives. **Three attractors** do this most reliably: **the sexual, the threatening, and the different.** Each works, but each is conditional; misuse backfires.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Three Attractors** — sex, threat, difference. Linked to reproduction, harm-avoidance, and the orienting reflex (environmental change).
- **Orienting Response (O-wave)** — ancestral attention hijack. Pavlov called it the "investigatory reflex." Any change in surroundings triggers brain-wide evaluation, superseding other operations. Also explains why walking through doorways makes you forget what you came for.
- **Dread Risks** — risky actions taken to avoid a *perceived* threat that is actually safer than the chosen alternative. Attention makes the dreaded option feel more probable than it is.
- **Fit-in vs. Stand-out (safety vs. mating).** Threat primes → want crowd ("don't be left out"). Sex primes → want distinction ("be one of the few"). The mismatch is why the same ad can soar in one slot and tank in the next.

## Attractor 1: The Sexual

**Works on:** products bought for sexually-related purposes — cosmetics, body scents, form-fitting clothing. Only 8 of Ad Age's top-100 20th-century campaigns used sex; it fails for soft drinks, detergents, appliances.

**Worked example — the Valentine Street study.** Middle-aged French men, asked for directions to "Valentine Street" (vs. "Martin Street") a few minutes earlier, were ~2x more likely to intervene when a pretty 19-year-old then claimed four thugs had stolen her phone. The Valentine's-Day association pre-suaded the bravado.

**Rule:** physical attractiveness alone doesn't capture attention — unless the viewer is already *in the market* for a partner. Students in romantic relationships who noticed attractive others were the most likely to break up two months later (stronger predictor than reported love, satisfaction, or desired duration).

## Attractor 2: The Threatening

**Threat pulls attention more reliably than pleasure.** From infancy. Produces staring-at-car-wrecks, blow-'em-up movie sales, dread-risk behavior.

**Dread risk example — post-9/11:** media coverage drove many Americans off airplanes onto highways. ~1,600 extra road deaths in the following year — ~6x the fatalities of the one US commercial crash. Replicated in London post-7/7: tube use dropped, bike injuries spiked. Attention → dread → riskier choice.

**Fear-based appeals work, but need a fix:** high fear alone triggers denial ("my grandfather smoked and lived to 80"). Pair the fear with a **clear, available, actionable step** — a workshop, a help line, a change-of-habit program. Dutch hypoglycemia study: patients given scary health news *plus* workshop info were 4x more likely to sign up than patients given a milder message.

**Formula:** scare + specific actionable remedy = behavior change. Scare alone = denial.

## Attractor 3: The Different

**Change itself commands attention** (orienting response). Pavlov's dogs stopped salivating whenever a new stimulus — a visitor, Pavlov entering the room — entered the lab. The investigatory reflex supersedes all other operations.

**Four practical implications:**
1. **Scene cuts in video.** The Annenberg producer's insight: in TV/film, *shots* carry content and *cuts* direct attention. A well-placed cut instigates an orienting response at the exact feature you want remembered. TV advertisers have *abused* this — raising cut-frequency 50%+ indiscriminately. Result: higher total attention, *less* persuasion. Death by a thousand cuts.
2. **Novelty in static media.** Print, billboards, email — can't cut. So differentiate: make the appeal distinctive (original, unfamiliar, surprising). Set an item apart from competitors and attention + favor attach.
3. **The decoy-by-contrast effect.** Northwestern study: sofa Dream (soft, less durable) vs. Titan (sturdy). Direct pairing: Titan won 58%. Add three irrelevant sofas that all had Titan-like durability. Now the Dream stood out as the *soft* one — and won 77%. Adding weak competitors *that share a feature* makes the odd-one-out win. Distinctiveness → attention → importance.
4. **Media buying.** Place "largest-selling" / crowd-based ads during threatening programming (crime dramas, news). Place "stand out from the crowd" / distinctiveness ads during romantic/comedy content. Most advertisers don't; most leave money on the table.

## Mental Models
- **Attention follows survival.** Reproduction, danger, and change-detection are the three oldest reasons an organism redirects focus. The three attractors are those three motivations.
- **Fear without agency → denial. Fear with a path → action.** The lever isn't fear volume; it's path clarity.
- **Context sets the appeal.** The same prospect, same ad, flips motivation based on what played in the 60 seconds before. The opener *is* the medium.
- **"Don't worry, it was the damned doorway."** The forgetting-at-the-doorway finding is the orienting response doing its daily work.

## Anti-patterns
- **Using sex to sell a dishwasher.** Advertisers do it; it doesn't work. Match attractor to product's actual purpose.
- **Scaring without a solution.** Triggers denial. Add an actionable step *in the same message.*
- **Dread-risk behavior in yourself.** Post-event, check your intuition against base rates — the dreaded option is often safer than the one you're switching to.
- **Over-cutting video.** More cuts ≠ more persuasion. Use cuts to direct attention to the best feature, then stop.
- **Scheduling ad spots by brand-conflict only.** Match emotional context to message type — threat content for crowd appeals, mating content for distinction appeals.
- **Running the "Ford best-selling" ad during a romance movie.** Opposite of what the audience's current state wants to hear.

## Worked Example — The Valentine Street Study (Guille / Guéguen)
A pretty 19-year-old approached random middle-aged lone men and pointed to four thugs: *"They stole my phone. Could you get it back for me?"* Baseline sample: 20% helped. Sample primed minutes earlier by a different woman asking for "Valentine Street" directions: ~40% helped. The Valentine's-Day connotation pre-suaded romantic bravado, inflating willingness to take real physical risk for a stranger.

## Key Takeaways
1. Three attractors do the attention work for you: sexual, threatening, different. Each has costly misuse modes.
2. Sex only sells sex-adjacent categories. Everything else is noise.
3. Fear works when it's coupled to an action. Fear alone is denial fuel.
4. Change beats sameness for attention — but only usefully-directed change (cuts, novelty, decoys-by-contrast).
5. Match the appeal to the audience's current emotional state. The pre-rolled TV show sets the frame.
6. Attention is zero-sum: a cut toward your strongest feature is a cut away from all others.
