# Chapter 3: What Do You Know?

## Core Idea
When someone argues from a position of certainty, don't fight the conclusion — question the basis. "What do you know about…?" forces them to show their evidence; often there isn't any.

## The Magic Words
> **"What do you know about…?"**

## Why It Works
- Strong opinions require a base of knowledge. Most don't have one.
- The question is non-aggressive — it sounds genuinely curious.
- It gives the other person a face-saving way to downgrade their certainty: "well, not much, actually."
- Either you learn their argument's real basis (so you can respond precisely), or they realize it was unfounded.

## When to Use
- "I know best" people lecturing you with their opinions.
- Confrontations that are heading into argument territory.
- When you need to shift them from certainty → doubt before you can introduce your idea.

## How
1. Don't challenge the conclusion. Challenge the knowledge behind it.
2. Fill the blank with the specific domain their opinion rests on.
3. Listen fully — don't rebut yet.
4. If they have real evidence, position your point in contrast to it. If they don't, they'll usually soften on their own.

## Worked Examples
- "What do you know about us, our business, and the way we do things differently?"
- "What do you know about everything that has changed since [event]?"
- "What do you know about how things really work here?"
- "What do you know about the benefits of [product sector]?"

## Anti-patterns
- **Delivered with a smirk or sarcasm.** Now it's a trap and they'll dig in.
- **Used on experts who actually know the domain.** You'll get a lecture — use "just out of curiosity" instead (ch23).
- **Immediately countering their answer.** The whole point is the softening — let their answer do the work.

## Key Takeaways
1. In arguments the loser rarely cooperates afterward — reframe so nobody has to lose.
2. "Either everybody wins, or everybody loses" is the operative mood.
3. Shifting certainty to doubt is almost always a prerequisite for changing a mind.
