# Chapter 9: You Have Three Options

## Core Idea
People hate feeling manipulated and want to feel like the decision was theirs. "As I see it, you have three options…" preserves their agency while letting you stack the choices so your preferred outcome is the path of least resistance.

## The Magic Words
> **"As I see it, you have three options…"**
> Close with: **"Of those three options, what's going to be easier for you?"**

## Why It Works
- **Rhythm of three.** Three options feel complete; two feels binary; four or more feels confusing.
- **Last-position weight.** The brain stacks value on the last option in a list and builds toward it as you speak.
- **Appearance of impartiality.** You're "just presenting the options" — but you built the menu.
- **"Easier for you"** is the closer — labor is the dimension on which your pick wins.

## When to Use
- Someone who likes your offer but is stuck.
- Classic objections: "I need to think about it," "I'm not sure."
- Any moment when they need help narrowing down and staying in control.

## How (the stack)
1. **Option 1 — the hard version of status-quo-alternative.** List the full labor involved.
2. **Option 2 — do nothing.** Make it sound passive and unacceptable.
3. **Option 3 — your offer.** Make it feel light and natural — the path of least resistance.
4. Close with: **"Of those three options, what's going to be easier for you?"**

## Worked Example (from the book)
Context: someone on the fence about joining your business opportunity.

> "So, you're currently in a job you kind of hate. You're not enjoying it so much, the hours are long, it's keeping you away from your family, and the money is nowhere near what you'd like it to be. We have shown you a business opportunity and you like it, yet you are not sure exactly what to do.
>
> As I see it, you have three options.
>
> **First**, you could look for another job, work on your résumé, send out applications, go through interviews, and work through that entire process to perhaps find another employer offering a similar package and more than likely expecting the same kind of work for the same return.
>
> **Second**, you could do absolutely nothing, stay exactly where you are right now, accept that your current circumstances are as good as it gets, and just suck it up.
>
> **Or third**, you could give this a try, work it alongside what you're doing right now, and see how far you go.
>
> Of those three options, what's going to be easier for you?"

## Anti-patterns
- **Four options.** Cognitive overload, people pick none.
- **Making option 3 sound hard.** Defeats the whole stack.
- **Closing with "which do you want?"** Instead of "what's easier for you?" — different levers; "easier" keyed to effort is the winning one.
- **Telling them which to pick.** Don't name your pick. Let the stack do the work.

## Key Takeaways
1. Decision help is the real product — most people are stuck because no one helps them narrow.
2. Order matters more than content: last option wins by default.
3. "Easier" is the closer-question that beats "better" or "preferred."
