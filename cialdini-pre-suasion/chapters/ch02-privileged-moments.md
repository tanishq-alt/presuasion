# Chapter 2: Privileged Moments

## Core Idea
Frequently, the factor most likely to determine a person's choice is not the one that *counsels most wisely* in the situation — it's the one that's been **elevated in attention** (and thereby in privilege) at the moment of decision. You can channel attention with a single question.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Privileged Moment** — a time-limited window after a pre-suasive opener when attention is channeled onto a selected concept and the listener is unusually vulnerable to aligned requests.
- **Positive Test Strategy** — when deciding if a possibility is correct, people hunt for *hits* (confirmations) and not for *misses* (disconfirmations). It is easier to register the presence of something than its absence.
- **Single-Chute Question** — a question framed in one direction only ("are you unhappy?" / "are you adventurous?") that funnels the respondent's memory search down one chute, biasing the self-concept they report.
- **Channeled Attention Model of Influence** — the "revolutionary" alternative to the traditional change-their-attitudes model. You don't need to change beliefs, attitudes, or experiences — only *what is prominent in their mind at the moment of decision.*
- **Attentional Blink** — ~half-second mental dead spot during a shift of focus, during which newly highlighted information can't be consciously registered.

## Key Concepts
- **Channeled attention is ruthless.** It not only promotes the focal aspect of a situation but *suppresses competing aspects* — even critically important ones (e.g. the fraud risk of giving your email to a stranger).
- **"Pay attention" is literal** — attention has a cost: attention lost to everything else. The mind holds one conscious track at a time; multitasking is rapid alternation with an attentional blink tax per switch.
- **Two-sided questions** ("How satisfied **or dissatisfied** are you?") invite even-handed consultation. **Single-sided questions bias the result.**
- **Reducing distance to an object makes it seem more worthwhile.** One study: shoppers who envisioned moving toward a snack container liked it more and would pay >4x as much.

## Mental Models
- **The palm-reading paradox.** Cialdini read the same homeowner's palm twice — once called him "stubborn," once "flexible" — and he agreed both times. The trait named funnels the memory search. Whatever you prime, people confirm.
- **Sherlock Holmes vs. the rest of us.** Holmes attends equally to what *didn't* happen ("the dog that didn't bark"). Most people only look for presences.
- **Erickson lowering his voice** when a truck went by, so the patient had to lean *toward* him to hear the crucial point — the forward posture itself signals "this is important" and the patient weights it accordingly.
- **The CD-player brain.** Only one track plays at a time. Any other arrangement produces noise.

## Anti-patterns
- **Answering single-chute survey questions.** They stack the deck. Decline, or ask the pollster for the two-sided form.
- **Being "unhappy" recruited by cults.** Cult recruiters open with "Are you unhappy?" — it's not a screen, it's a *recruiting* device that focuses the prospect onto dissatisfactions in the privileged moment.
- **Visible distraction during conversation** (page turns, clicking keys). Signals low valuation of the speaker — a self-inflicted "demotion."
- **Making a request in neutral time rather than privileged time.** Same request, different moment, different compliance rate.

## Worked Example — The Email-Address Experiment
Bolkan & Andersen approached people in a mall for their emails to receive a sample of a new soft drink.
- **Cold ask:** 33% complied.
- **Pre-suasive opener: "Do you consider yourself to be somebody who is adventurous and likes to try new things?"** 97% said yes. Of those, **75.7%** then gave their email address.

Note two things:
1. The ludicrous rate of "yes, I'm adventurous" (97% — because single-chute).
2. These are the same people who, under normal caution, would refuse email to a stranger in a world of phishing — attention channeled to "adventurous" suppressed the risk signal entirely.

Companion study with the survey ask: baseline 29% → after **"Do you consider yourself a helpful person?"** → **77.3%** compliance.

## Key Takeaways
1. What's elevated in attention at the moment of decision usually beats what would counsel most wisely.
2. One well-framed question can channel the self-concept the respondent reports, and the compliance that follows it.
3. Attention is zero-sum. Priming one concept actively *suppresses* competitors.
4. The decision isn't being made on the full evidence available — it's being made on the slice of evidence currently in focus.
5. Resisting pre-suasion starts with noticing single-chute framing and asking: *what's the two-sided version of this question?*
6. If you want them to attend, make them physically orient (lean in, approach). The body signals the mind what matters.
