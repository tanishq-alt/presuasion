# Chapter 4: What's Focal Is Causal

## Core Idea
Focused attention does a second thing beyond inflating importance (ch3): it assigns **causality**. We allot special attention to true causes around us, so the reverse chains automatically — if we see ourselves giving attention to something, we treat it as a cause. Salient factors come to seem causal even when they aren't. Channeled attention therefore pre-suades by pre-answering the "why" question.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **What's Focal Is Causal effect** — whatever is visually, psychologically, or narratively prominent is assumed to drive the outcome. "Practically unmovable" and "automatic" across male/female observers, delayed judgments, varied contexts.
- **The Helping Norm / Norm of Social Responsibility** — people help in proportion to perceived need. Visible need triggers obligation.
- **"The Romance of Leadership"** — leaders are assigned causal weight far beyond their actual impact on group outcomes. Workforce quality, business systems, and market conditions drive more of corporate results — yet the CEO is salient, so attributed outsize responsibility (and compensation ~200x average employee).

## Key Concepts
- **Money looks causal because it's salient.** The economic worldview assumes self-interest is primary — in part because monetary exchanges draw the eye. Hidden motivators (obligation, personal values, morals) are often the real drivers but go unnoticed.
- **False confessions** are produced by the same mechanism. The jury sees the suspect talking (salient) and judges them causal of their words — even when questioning tactics produced them.
- **Camera angle creates causality.** Taylor's and Lassiter's research: whichever face is more visible is judged more influential. Changing camera angle changes who seems responsible.

## Three Levers (illustrated)

### 1. Monetary Offers Reveal Need, Not Just Incentive
Oberholzer-Gee offered people cash to let him cut in line. More money → higher compliance (50% at $1, 65% at $3, 75%+ at $5 and $10). But *almost no one took the money.* Larger sums signaled greater *need*, triggering the helping norm. Money looked causal; social obligation actually was.

### 2. Lottery Numbers from the Tylenol Case
Autumn 1982: cyanide-laced Tylenol capsules killed seven people. Johnson & Johnson publicized the two production lot numbers on the affected bottles: **2,880 and 1,910**. Within days, players in RI, NH, and PA lottery systems bet so heavily on those numbers that states had to halt wagers above "maximum liability levels." Ordinary unlucky numbers — associated with grisly death — became *expectation-raising* because the publicity made them focal. Focal → causal → "they'll win."

### 3. The Peter Reilly Case — False Confession via Focus
Eighteen-year-old Peter Reilly came home in 1973 to find his mother brutally murdered. He waived his right to a lawyer. Over 16 hours of interrogation by a rotating team, a false polygraph claim, and suggestions that he had "repressed" the memory, he was pushed to "imagine" the act. Eventually he confessed. He recanted the next day; too late. Convicted. Two years later, hidden evidence found in the prosecutor's files (after the prosecutor died) established Reilly's innocence.

Arthur Miller (whose *The Crucible* used Salem witch-trial interrogation transcripts) later noted: the questioning patterns in Salem 1692, 1950s HUAC hearings, 1960s Chinese Cultural Revolution, and the Reilly case were *identical*. These extraction techniques evolved convergently; they work.

## The Camera-Angle Problem
Most police-interrogation recordings film from behind the officer, onto the suspect's face. The suspect is more visually salient → observers (judges, jurors, police themselves) assign the suspect greater causal responsibility for the confession and greater guilt. This bias survived every control Lassiter tried — single vs repeat viewings, intellectually deep vs shallow observers, pre-warnings about bias, law enforcement professionals, judges.

**Only one thing fixes it: change the camera angle.** Side-view erases bias. Over-the-suspect-shoulder (onto interrogator) *reverses* it — observers judge the interrogator coercive.

## Worked Example — Cialdini's Operational Advice
*If you are ever brought to a police station to assist with a serious crime and begin to feel the session is turning into an interrogation:*

1. **Option A:** stop the session, request a lawyer. Cost: suspicion from appearing to hide something.
2. **Option B:** continue to clear your name. Cost: you may be subjected to centuries-refined techniques that produce confessions, sometimes from the innocent.
3. **If you choose B, do two specific things:**
   - **Find the camera** (usually above and behind the police officer).
   - **Move your chair** so your face and the officer's are equally focal in the recording.
4. Cialdini's quip: "If you demand a lawyer and the police push back, blame me. Blame this book."

## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming money is the real cause** because it's salient. Hidden motivators (obligation, values) often outweigh it.
- **Betting on focal-but-arbitrary objects** (lottery numbers from headlines). Attention ≠ causality.
- **Trusting over-the-shoulder confession tapes.** The camera angle is the verdict.
- **Attributing results to the visible leader.** Workforce, systems, market — they do most of the lifting. The CEO is salient and gets the credit (or blame).
- **Imagining yourself doing the crime** at an interrogator's request. The imagination becomes memory, becomes confession.

## Key Takeaways
1. What's focal is causal — the effect is automatic, universal, resistant to counter-information.
2. Causality perception follows the eye: shift the eye, shift the perceived cause.
3. Visible stimuli (money, celebrities, CEOs, numbers in the news) are systematically over-weighted as causes.
4. Interrogation techniques exploit this: salient suspect + hidden questioner → "the suspect must have done it."
5. To protect yourself: control what's framed into attention — whether at a crime scene camera, a boardroom pitch, or a public explanation of results.
6. The persuasive dream: get recipients to devote attention to your case *before* evaluating it. Focus pre-answers "important?" and "causal?" favorably.
