# Chapter 10: Six Main Roads to Change — Broad Boulevards as Smart Shortcuts

## Core Idea
Pre-suasive openers are most powerful when they channel attention toward one of six universal principles of influence: **reciprocation, liking, social proof, authority, scarcity, consistency.** Each one is a genuine decision shortcut that normally steers people correctly, so highlighting it (both *before* and *during* a message) heightens compliance.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Six Universal Principles of Influence** (from *Influence*, now extended pre-suasively) — reciprocation, liking, social proof, authority, scarcity, consistency. Chapter 11 adds unity as a seventh.
- **The Core Motives Model of Social Influence** (Gregory Neidert) — different stages of a relationship suit different principles.
- **Credible Authority** — expertise *plus* trustworthiness. The second is often the missing lever.
- **Weakness-before-strength tactic** — mentioning a drawback first establishes honesty; the subsequent strength then gets believed. Elizabeth I: "I know I have the body of a weak and feeble woman; **but** I have the heart of a king."
- **Meaningful + Unexpected + Customized** — the three multipliers of a gift's reciprocity return.

## The Six Principles

### 1. Reciprocation — "People say yes to those they owe."
- Rule for reciprocation is taught in every culture from toddlerhood; freeloaders are universally shamed.
- Candy-store gift: 42% more purchases after a free piece at entry.
- Dutch survey: payment-with-the-letter more effective than payment-after-participation.
- Hotel-towel reuse: **47% more effective** when the hotel donated *first* (vs. promising to donate after).
- Three features that multiply reciprocity returns:
  - **Meaningful** — not expensive; just significant to recipient. (New Jersey restaurant: chocolate basket at the end of the meal; one piece → +3.3% tip; two pieces → +14.1%.)
  - **Unexpected** — chocolate basket, then walking away, then returning with a second chocolate → +21.3% tip.
  - **Customized** — the biggest multiplier. Fast-food greeting + free yogurt (food-related, specific to visitor's need) → +24% spending; free keychain → only +12%. **The CIA + Viagra story**: operative learned an Afghan patriarch had four wives and was exhausted; next visit brought four Viagra tablets. Tribe chief's gratitude translated into intelligence on Taliban movements.
  - **Abu Jandal (bin Laden's bodyguard) and sugar-free cookies** — interrogators noticed he was diabetic and left cookies uneaten; served him sugar-free cookies. He stopped lecturing, started talking, named seven 9/11 hijackers.

### 2. Liking — "People say yes to those they like." But **the real rule is: people say yes to those who they feel *like them*.**
- Two best levers to generate liking: **similarities** and **compliments**.
- **Similarities:** language style, facial expressions, interests, nationality, sports team. Waitresses coached to mimic customer verbal style **doubled their tips**. Negotiators mimicking opponents got better outcomes. Salespeople mimicking language and posture sold more.
- **Compliments:** "I can live two months on a good compliment" (Twain). Hair salon stylists saying "any hairstyle would look good on you" → +37% tips. Even obvious/preprinted flattery with ulterior motive still works (Chinese college student flyer study; computer-generated flattery).
- **Cialdini's correction to the classic sales rule:** it's not "get customers to like you." It's "show customers you *genuinely like them.*" People trust that those who like them will steer them right. *"People don't care how much you know until they know how much you care."*

### 3. Social Proof — "People model the behavior of similar others."
Two components drive change:
- **Validity** — if many similar others do X, X seems more morally and practically correct.
  - 80% became more acceptant of torture after learning their peers supported it.
  - Working professionals told most people try to overcome stereotypes became more resistant to stereotypes in their own work conduct.
  - Beijing restaurants labeling items "most popular" → each labeled dish became 13–20% more popular.
  - Dutch high schoolers told their peers eat fruit for health → +35% fruit consumption.
  - Industry pollution-ranking disclosure → heavy polluters reduce emissions ~30%.
- **Feasibility** — if others are doing it, it's clearly *doable.* This is why social proof beat "save money" messaging in Cialdini's own home energy-conservation study (3.5x more savings than any other appeal).

### 4. Authority — "The messenger is the message." But only certain messengers.
- Not someone *in* authority (hierarchical) — someone *an* authority (expertise).
- Brain-scan study: when experts advise economic decisions, evaluation-region brain activity *flatlines*. People stop evaluating and follow.
- **Credible authority** = expertise **+ trustworthiness.**
- **Trustworthiness instant-upgrade trick:** mention a weakness *first*. Perceived truthfulness is now in place; strengths that follow are believed more.
  - Trial attorneys who admit weakness first win more often.
  - Candidates who start with something positive about an opponent gain voting intentions.
  - Ads acknowledging a drawback before strengths boost sales significantly.
  - Optimal: use a transition word (**but, however, yet**) and make the strength actively undercut the weakness's relevance.
- **Elizabeth I at Tilbury (1588):** *"I know I have the body of a weak and feeble woman; **but** I have the heart of a king, and a king of England, too!"* Cheers so loud they had to be quieted. Thirteen years later, "Golden Speech" to a hostile Parliament used the same device.

### 5. Scarcity — "We want more of what we can have less of."
- Crumbs cupcakes went from $4 to $250 online when the chain announced closing.
- iPhone-release story: woman trades her $2,800 Louis Vuitton bag for 2 spots up in line because supply was tight.
- **Loss aversion powers it.** The brokerage mentor's maxim: wake a multimillionaire at 5am and say "you'll gain $20K now" — he'll slam down. Say "you'll lose $20K if you don't act" — he'll thank you.
- **Purchase limits ("Only X per customer")** more than doubled sales on seven products in a grocery chain. Any access constraint raises perceived worth.

### 6. Consistency — "We align with our prior commitments."
- Public prior stance → private future stance pulled to match.
- Prayer study: romantic partners who prayed daily for their partner's well-being were less likely to be unfaithful.
- **Honesty pledge** at the *start* of an odometer-reading form (vs. the end) reduces misreporting.
- Getting voters to vote in one election raises likelihood they vote in the next.
- Blood-drive wording change: *"We'll mark you on the list as coming then. Thank you."* → *"We'll mark you on the list as coming then, okay? [pause] Thank you."* Donor attendance rose from 70% to **82.4%**.
- **Leverage existing commitments.** The 2013 marriage-equality legal strategy targeted Justice Anthony Kennedy by threading his own prior language — "human dignity," "individual liberty," "personal freedoms/rights" — into the months-long public campaign and in-court arguments. His majority opinions in favor of marriage equality then leaned on exactly those three concepts.

## Neidert's Core Motives Model — Which Principle at Which Stage?

| Stage | Main goal | Best principles |
|---|---|---|
| **Stage 1 — Cultivating positive association** | Make them favorable to *you* | **Reciprocation** (give first, meaningful/unexpected/customized) + **Liking** (similarities, genuine compliments) |
| **Stage 2 — Reducing uncertainty** | Make the decision feel *wise* | **Social Proof** (peers do it) + **Authority** (experts endorse it) |
| **Stage 3 — Motivating action** | Get the actual yes | **Consistency** (remind of their prior public stance) + **Scarcity** (what's lost by inaction) |

## Mental Models
- **Shortcuts work most of the time.** Expertise, popularity, scarcity, prior commitment — these are *correct* heuristics on average. That's why channeling attention to them pre-suades legitimately.
- **Give first.** The default commercial exchange (benefit after action) underperforms reciprocity opened with up-front giving.
- **Trust is a precursor, not a bonus.** The weakness-first trick isn't humility theater — it's the fastest known way to install trust.
- **Consistency is self-signed contracts.** Existing commitments are pre-fetched. One reminder launches them.

## Anti-patterns
- **Reciprocating with cheap, generic gifts.** No lift. Needs meaning + surprise + customization.
- **Faking similarity or compliments.** Insincerity degrades the lever and burns future rounds.
- **Claiming authority without evidence.** The brain scans for expertise cues; without them the flat-line effect doesn't fire.
- **Burying all drawbacks until the end.** Opposite of the trust-building move.
- **Creating artificial scarcity.** Legal in degree, but detection destroys trust. Use genuine constraints.
- **Asking for commitments and then not reminding of them.** The prior yes is only as useful as the next recall.

## Worked Examples
- **CIA + Viagra** (reciprocity, customized): four tablets, one per wife. Next visit: Taliban movements and supply routes.
- **Abu Jandal + sugar-free cookies**: meaningful + unexpected + customized gift → turned interrogation.
- **Marriage equality + Kennedy's own language**: consistency via language-threading in the media + oral arguments.
- **Elizabeth I's "Golden Speech"**: weakness → *"but / yet"* → strength that undercuts the weakness.
- **Beijing restaurants labeling "most popular"**: social proof lift of 13–20%.

## Key Takeaways
1. There are six universal persuasive principles — these are the *content* that channeled attention should touch.
2. Pre-suasion works best when the opener points to one of these six.
3. Reciprocity: give first, meaningful + unexpected + customized.
4. Liking: show them *you* like *them*, via similarities and real compliments.
5. Social proof: use peers for validity, and feasibility (strongest lever most communicators under-use).
6. Authority: admit a weakness first; expertise + trustworthiness together.
7. Scarcity: frame as loss, not gain. Imposed limits multiply value.
8. Consistency: make small public prior commitments; later remind them.
9. Different stages (association → uncertainty → action) favor different principles. Match principle to stage.
