# Chapter 20: Enough

## Core Idea
When someone is deliberating over quantity, don't ask them to choose — ask a direct closed question using the larger number: "Would [bigger number] be enough for you?" Yes is the path of least resistance.

## The Magic Words
> **"Would [N] be enough for you?"**

## Why It Works
- **Closed yes/no with a presupposition.** You've picked the number for them; all that's left is yes.
- **"Enough" anchors sufficiency.** Saying no to "enough" means admitting it's not enough — which pushes them to agree or go higher.
- **Simplifies the deliberation.** Instead of weighing 4 vs 8, they weigh "yes or no" to 8.
- **Open choice splits 50/50; direct question with the bigger number swings it much higher.**

## When to Use
- Quantity decisions (how many units, hours, seats, months, bottles).
- Upsell moments where you want the larger option without arguing for it.
- Any decision between two numbers where the bigger one is viable.

## How
1. Identify the two plausible quantities they're weighing.
2. Ask: "Would [the larger one] be enough for you?"
3. Yes closes at the higher quantity. If no, you can pivot to "What would be enough?" and still be in control.

## Worked Examples
- Apples at grocery: between 4 and 8 → "Would eight apples be enough for you?" → yes.
- Drinking gel, 2 vs 3 bottles → "Would three bottles be enough for you?" → yes.
- Training sessions, 6 vs 10 → "Would ten sessions be enough to get you where you want to go?"

## The Broader Principle
> *In every set of circumstances in which you involve yourself in the decision-making process, you have the power to influence the actions of others.*

Consumers love being led to the right action. Not intervening *is* a decision — and usually the wrong one for them. One more unit per transaction, compounded across every conversation, is a huge effect.

## Anti-patterns
- **Choosing a wildly unrealistic larger number.** Breaks credibility.
- **Open comparative question** ("Do you want 4 or 8?"). Splits 50/50 and loses the lever.
- **Follow-up pressure if they say no.** Pivot gracefully: "What would be enough?"

## Key Takeaways
1. Direct closed yes/no questions beat open comparative ones for quantity decisions.
2. "Enough" is the word that anchors the frame.
3. Habit of use: on every transaction, ask the "enough" question with the larger option.
