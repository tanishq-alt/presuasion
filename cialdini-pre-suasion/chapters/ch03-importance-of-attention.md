# Chapter 3: The Importance of Attention . . . Is Importance

## Core Idea
Anything that draws focused attention to itself can lead observers to overestimate its importance. Whatever you put at the center of someone's attention, they will treat as if it mattered more. Kahneman's one-sentence summary of the core finding: **"Nothing in life is as important as you think it is while you are thinking about it."** The communicator who gets an audience to focus on a key element pre-loads that element with importance.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Focusing Illusion** (Kahneman) — whatever you're thinking about seems disproportionately important *while* you're thinking about it. The attention itself inflates the judged weight.
- **Agenda Setting** (Cohen; media theory) — the press rarely tells people *what* to think, but is "stunningly successful in telling them what to think about." Translation: coverage drives perceived importance of issues, not opinions about them.
- **Satisficing** (Herbert Simon) — "satisfy + suffice." Decision makers pick the first *good-enough* option rather than optimizing. Makes unitary focus even more decisive — whatever gets evaluated first often wins.
- **Consider-the-opposite** — a debiasing procedure: force the two questions "What future events could make this plan go wrong?" and "What would happen to us if it did?" Companies using such processes get a 5–7% ROI advantage.

## Three Pre-suasive Levers in This Chapter

### 1. Managing the Background
Background cues channel attention without conscious notice.
- **Mandel & Johnson online furniture study:** clouds in the landing-page wallpaper → visitors prioritized *comfort* and bought more expensive comfortable sofas. Pennies → prioritized *price* and bought cheap. Most participants denied the wallpaper had any effect.
- **Banner ads** flashed every few seconds beside an article — never consciously noticed, never recalled — were liked *more* the more they appeared. Recognition/recall drastically underestimates their effectiveness.
- **Negative background:** children in classrooms near elevated subway tracks or flight paths test lower; scores jump after sound-baffling. Classrooms with heavily decorated walls (posters, maps, artwork) reduce young children's science-test scores. Background steals attention bandwidth.

### 2. Inviting Favorable (Unitary) Evaluation
If your offering has merit, make them evaluate it *alone*, not against rivals.
- Single-brand camera surveys ("rate the qualities of Canon") raised both ratings and purchase intent; the effect dropped when respondents first considered rivals (Nikon, Olympus, etc.).
- Research across hotels, restaurants, consumer products, and charities: single-chute evaluation raised willingness to support.
- **Operational use:** satisfaction surveys that ask you to rate *this provider only* harvest focused favorable attention as a side-bonus.
- Mirror effect at the strategic level: paint-company managers given one of four possible solutions to a sales slump were more likely to recommend *that* solution — regardless of which one it was.

### 3. Shifting the Task at Hand
Change what the attention-bearers are *doing* and you change what they attend to.
- **The embedded reporter program (Iraq 2003).** Reporters embedded with combat units produced emotionally gripping, soldier-focused coverage — 93% of embedded stories came from the troops' perspective. "Weapons of mass destruction" appeared in just 2% of all stories. Front-page coverage was 71% embedded. The broader strategic justification for the war — the weakest leg — barely registered. Attention was conducted away from the "whys" of the fight and onto the "whos and hows."
- Officials didn't necessarily plan this effect; it emerged from the task shift. The focusing illusion did the rest.

## Mental Models
- **"Pay attention" literally — attention is a currency.** Spent on A, it's unavailable to B. When media or a prompt spends your attention on an issue, you default to importance ranking.
- **Rhesus monkeys and status.** Monkeys will *pay* (in forfeited food rewards) just to look at high-status colony members. All kinds of species evolved to attend to what matters most — which is why *channeling* attention hacks that mechanism.
- **"There's no such thing as bad publicity"** is partly right: attention is itself a form of valuation. Cialdini revised his view of the Hollywood cliché after Tiger Woods (counter-example) alongside the many celebrities who thrive on sheer presence.
- **Molecular vs molar task framing.** Embedded reporters were given a molecular task (describe this soldier today); their coverage reflected it. Shift the grain, shift the attention, shift the perceived importance.

## Anti-patterns
- **Pulling attention to a weak idea.** Backfires — more consideration → more polarized rejection. The lever only works for good cases.
- **Deciding on a single option without consider-the-opposite.** Satisficing dressed up as decisiveness. Costs 5–7% ROI.
- **Ignoring background.** Noisy classrooms, cluttered walls, or busy app UIs steal cognitive bandwidth from the figure you want in focus.
- **Assuming you weren't influenced.** Mandel & Johnson subjects *refused to believe* clouds/pennies affected them. Denial of the lever is the lever's natural companion.

## Worked Example — The Queen's Phone
At a Golden Jubilee reception, a woman's phone began ringing just as she met Queen Elizabeth II. Frozen with embarrassment, she stared at her bag; the royal eyes also fixed on it. Elizabeth leaned forward: *"You should answer that, dear. It might be someone important."* The Queen — the most important person in the room by any measure — ceded importance to an unknown caller because the phone had seized focus. The focusing illusion is universal; royalty gets it too.

## Key Takeaways
1. Attention is the currency. Perceived importance is what it buys.
2. If your offer is strong, make the evaluator face it *alone* — never in parallel comparison with equally strong rivals.
3. Background cues shape foreground judgment even when consciously invisible.
4. Media coverage doesn't change minds, it changes agendas — which is enough.
5. To debias your own decisions: pre-mortem (consider-the-opposite). Force the failure questions before committing.
6. Attention channeling only *amplifies* the case you have. Weak cases get worse under scrutiny.
